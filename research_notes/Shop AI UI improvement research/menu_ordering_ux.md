# Menu-Browsing & Food-Ordering UI/UX Best Practices for Diner-Facing QR-Menu Apps

Scope note: Shop AI is a QR-menu assistant on a 390px phone viewport with no checkout/payment — diners browse, filter, tag "picks," and flag staff or ask an AI assistant. Findings below are filtered for relevance to that model; where a source's pattern assumes a shopping cart with payment, this is flagged explicitly and translated into a "flag for staff" equivalent in the Inferences.

## Mobile menu browsing: search/filter placement, category nav (tabs vs. pills vs. sticky headers), image- vs. text-forward cards at 390px

### Takeaway
On a 390px phone, filters and category navigation must sit "above the fold" and stay reachable via sticky/collapsible placement rather than a separate screen; category counts should stay in the 5–8 range with plain, guest-facing labels; and dish images should be used selectively (bestsellers) rather than uniformly, since full photo-forward grids are costly to source and load.

### Cited Findings
- QR-menu tabletop UX should open instantly with no splash screen or app download, and be designed specifically for the phone's screen width rather than a repurposed PDF/desktop layout — "PDFs require pinch-zooming, don't reflow for phones, and are notoriously slow to load on mobile data." — [Ogent](https://restaurant.ogent.ai/qr-code-menu-best-practices-for-restaurants/)
- Recommended category navigation should be "above the fold" without requiring scrolling or a hamburger menu, limited to 5–8 categories, ordered by natural meal progression (starters → soups → mains → sides → drinks → desserts), and named "the way guests talk" (e.g., "Small chops" not "Amuse-bouche & Appetisers"). — [Dineazi](https://dineazi.com/resources/qr-menu-design-tips)
- Most restaurant menus work best with five to seven categories and fewer than seven items per category; "past seven, guests stop reading sections and start skipping them." — [UpMenu](https://www.upmenu.com/blog/menu-categories/)
- NN/g's menu-design guidance: keep primary navigation visible rather than hidden behind a hamburger menu wherever screen space allows ("the hamburger menu is a necessary evil on smaller screens"); group closely related items into distinct local sections to avoid users "pogo-sticking" between distant menu areas; avoid multilevel cascading menus beyond two tiers because they become frustrating; add small icons/images only as a supplement to clear text labels, never as a replacement, for accessibility; ensure tap targets are large enough to avoid accidental selection; and avoid hover-only interactions since they exclude touchscreen users. — [NN/g, Menu-Design Checklist](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/menu-design/)
- For dish-card photography specifically: photograph only the top 10–15 bestselling items with real restaurant food (not stock photos), compress images to under 200KB each, and keep descriptions to 1–2 lines using "sensory and functional cues" (heat level, "serves two") rather than long provenance storytelling — strategic photo placement is said to "elevate adjacent non-photographed items" by visual association. — [Dineazi](https://dineazi.com/resources/qr-menu-design-tips)
- Performance target: first meaningful menu content should load within 2–3 seconds on a 4G connection; slow loads cause bounce even when the QR scan itself worked fine. — [Dineazi](https://dineazi.com/resources/qr-menu-design-tips); corroborated generally by [Ogent](https://restaurant.ogent.ai/qr-code-menu-best-practices-for-restaurants/)
- Mobile filter-placement patterns, compared: **top bar** (horizontal, collapsing into dropdowns) suits simple, frequently used, short-label filters with high visibility but limited space; **sidebar** suits comprehensive, essential filter sets but reduces content width and has "limited filter discoverability" on mobile unless collapsible; **inline** placement (filters embedded directly in content) gives immediate feedback between filter and results but has lower visibility on dense pages; chip-style controls are best "for showing active selections visually" and enabling quick removal via an X icon, while dropdowns suit numerous options and support search. — [SetProduct](https://www.setproduct.com/blog/filter-ui-design)
- General mobile filter guidance: filters should be collapsible by default with a clearly visible expand affordance, and should be implemented as sticky elements so they remain reachable while scrolling; a persistent bottom-anchored filter control is easier to reach with thumbs than a top-right one. — [Onething Design](https://www.onething.design/post/best-filter-ux-ui-examples-saas-applications) (general filter guidance, not food-specific — noted as such)
- A DoorDash-focused UX case study found "5 out of 5 users interviewed" cited frustration specifically with navigating the restaurant menu, motivating a redesign of the menu page's information architecture — but the full write-up (which would detail the specific fixes) could not be retrieved (403 error on full fetch); only the problem statement is confirmed. — [UX Collective / Jo Zhouzheng, search snippet](https://uxdesign.cc/doordash-redesign-a-ux-case-study-559370cac697)
- Baymard Institute's Uber Eats case study catalogs "Food Menu," "Filtering Options + Restaurant List," "Sorting Tool," and "Product Page" as distinct benchmarked screen types across 26 mobile-web and 28 mobile-app pages (398 design elements total), confirming that leading apps treat menu, filter, sort, and item-detail as separate, purpose-built UI zones rather than one screen — but the specific critiques are paywalled and could not be retrieved. — [Baymard Institute](https://baymard.com/ux-benchmark/case-studies/uber-eats)

### Inferences
- For Shop AI's current layout (search bar + category pills + dietary pills all stacked at the top), pills are a reasonable choice for category nav per the tabs-vs-pills research (functionally interchangeable — "the main difference is in the way of presentation") — [Quora-sourced summary](https://www.quora.com/Whats-the-difference-between-pills-and-tabs-in-Twitter-Bootstrap) — but at 390px, stacking three distinct pill/search rows (search, category, dietary) risks pushing the dish list below the fold before the diner scrolls; making the category-pill row sticky while letting search/dietary filters collapse into an expandable control (per the SetProduct top-bar and Onething collapsible-filter patterns) would preserve "above the fold" category access without permanently consuming three rows of vertical space.
- Given the "5–8 categories, real-food photography only for bestsellers" pattern, Shop AI's `DishCard` list should not assume every dish needs a photo; a mixed image-forward (bestsellers/specials) + text-forward (rest of menu) layout is defensible and reduces asset/load burden, consistent with the 200KB/2-3-second load targets found above.
- The recurring "avoid jargon, name categories the way guests talk" guidance is a low-cost content fix independent of any code change.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve DoorDash's or Uber Eats' specific redesign solutions (both sources paywalled/blocked); only the underlying problem statements are confirmed. Grab and foodpanda-specific menu/category design write-ups were found in search snippets only (e.g., a foodpanda redesign case study on Medium) and were not deep-fetched, so no concrete cited pattern from those two apps is included here — treat any Grab/foodpanda claims as absent rather than assumed.
- No source directly benchmarked tabs vs. pills specifically for food-menu category navigation (only general UI-framework comparisons were found); the choice appears to be treated as a visual/branding decision rather than one with a proven usability edge either way in the sources retrieved.

## Dietary/allergen information display: icon systems, color coding, placement, legal-safe wording

### Takeaway
Allergen disclosure should be item-level (not a single page-bottom disclaimer), paired with a direct "ask staff" instruction, and icons should supplement rather than replace text labels; there is no single federal U.S. standard, but state laws (e.g., California) are moving toward mandatory item-level disclosure while explicitly not requiring "may contain" cross-contamination language.

### Cited Findings
- The FDA Food Code expects food establishments to inform customers about the nine major allergens (milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, sesame), but there is no single federal law mandating allergen disclosure on restaurant menus specifically — state laws are increasingly filling that gap. — [ADDE Act guide](https://adde.food-regulations.org/how-to-list-allergens-on-a-menu/)
- Allergen disclosure "is not a general disclaimer at the bottom of the page — it is item-level disclosure," and this requirement applies across all physical and digital menus, including app menus, website menus, menu boards, and ordering kiosks. — [ADDE Act guide](https://adde.food-regulations.org/how-to-list-allergens-on-a-menu/)
- California's new allergen disclosure law requires covered restaurant chains to comply with item-level disclosure; restaurant groups have published multi-step compliance plans in response. — [Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/many-restaurant-chains-must-comply-with-californias-new-allergen-disclosure-law); background/advocacy context in [California Law Review, "Fearless Dining"](https://www.californialawreview.org/print/fearless-dining)
- The California ADDE Act explicitly does NOT require "may contain" or "shared facility" precautionary language — that remains voluntary, not mandatory, under current legal consensus. — [ADDE Act guide](https://adde.food-regulations.org/restaurant-allergen-disclosure-requirements/)
- Sample compliant wording exists at the jurisdiction level: Massachusetts requires point-of-order language such as "Before placing your order, please inform your server if a person in your party has a food allergy." A commonly used general allergy warning reads: "Allergy Warning: Menu items may contain or come into contact with wheat, eggs, nuts and milk. Ask our staff for more information." — [Natick, MA municipal guidance](https://natickma.gov/DocumentCenter/View/5491/Certifications-Signage-and-Menu-Wording)
- Design guidance for allergen icon systems: icons should be "subtly embedded in the design's color palette while remaining clearly legible," and a menu should "force a consistent allergen method" — pick one of letters, icons, or a standard list, and apply it uniformly across every category rather than mixing conventions. — [EasyMenus](https://easymenus.eu/blog/en/menu-templates-for-allergen-friendly-restaurants)
- NN/g's general menu-design guidance (not allergen-specific but directly applicable): icons/images should be supplementary to clear text labels, never a replacement, for accessibility reasons. — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/menu-design/)
- Grubhub's dietary-filter research found that word choice materially reduces confusion — specifically, using "free" suffixes like "Milk-free" resolved ambiguity over whether a filter selection would include or exclude an ingredient, versus vaguer phrasing. — [Silvia Diff, Grubhub Dietary Filters case study](https://www.silviadiff.com/grubhub-dietary-filters)
- The same Grubhub research found dietary preferences work best when editable directly from the filter menu (not buried in account settings) and that some users want to save multiple dietary-preference sets (e.g., ordering for family members with different restrictions). — [Silvia Diff](https://www.silviadiff.com/grubhub-dietary-filters)
- A common industry pain point: people with allergies/intolerances struggle to find compatible items "especially when ordering remotely without easily being able to ask the restaurant staff for advice." — [Addictaco](https://addictaco.com/best-ux-practices-for-food-delivery-apps/)

### Inferences
- Because Shop AI is a QR-menu-at-the-table product (not remote delivery), it has a structural advantage the sources above call out as a delivery-app weakness: diners CAN ask staff directly. This suggests allergen tags on `DishCard`/detail view should pair every icon with explicit wording that reinforces this channel, e.g., "Contains peanuts — ask your waiter to confirm," which aligns with both the Massachusetts-style point-of-order language and the ADDE Act's item-level (not blanket-disclaimer) requirement.
- Given no source found supports blanket "may contain traces of" disclaimers as sufficient or required, and one source confirms it's legally voluntary, Shop AI should not rely on a single footer disclaimer as its main allergen safety UX — item-level tags per dish (which the current design already seems to have) are the correct pattern per the sources.
- The Grubhub "free"-suffix wording finding is directly actionable for Shop AI's dietary pills/tags: prefer "Dairy-free," "Gluten-free," "Nut-free" phrasing over ambiguous single-word tags.
- One consistent icon/text/color convention across the whole menu (not mixed conventions per category) is a concrete, low-effort audit item for Shop AI's current dietary/allergen pill system.

### Gaps
- No source was found addressing color-coding conventions specifically for allergen severity (e.g., red = severe allergen vs. yellow = dietary preference) — the sources found discuss icon/text consistency but not a color-coding standard. This should be treated as an open question, not inferred.
- No source directly confirmed how major food-delivery apps (DoorDash, Uber Eats specifically) place allergen info — card vs. detail view — with citable specifics; only general dietary-filter UX (Grubhub, Deliveroo) was found with citable detail.
- Could not verify whether Myanmar/local jurisdiction (relevant to Shop_Ai's likely deployment context) has its own allergen-disclosure requirements; all legal sourcing found is U.S.-specific (federal FDA Food Code, California, Massachusetts).

## "Picks"/shortlist UI (not a cart with payment): quantity adjustment and staff confirmation patterns

### Takeaway
The clearest analog in the literature is the wishlist-vs-cart distinction: a cart signals immediate transactional intent while a wishlist signals aspirational/save-for-later intent — Shop AI's "picks" list is functionally closer to a wishlist that gets verbally handed to staff, not a cart, and no source found describes a distinct third "flag for staff" pattern by name, so recommendations here are adapted from cart/wishlist research plus physical restaurant "call for service" conventions rather than a directly matching digital case study.

### Cited Findings
- The cart represents "high purchase intent and immediate needs," while the wishlist represents "aspirational interest, future planning, and product comparison" — different intents call for different design treatment (cart UX should minimize friction/urgency; wishlist UX should support longer-term browsing). — [NN/g, "Wishlist or shopping cart?"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wishlist-or-cart/); consistent summary at [GroPulse](https://gropulse.com/wishlist-vs-cart/)
- Recommended wishlist design patterns include surfacing the wishlist icon/entry point on product lists, product cards, and the main navigation — not just buried in a menu — so it stays discoverable. — [GroPulse](https://gropulse.com/wishlist-vs-cart/)
- Toast's Mobile Order & Pay lets guests scan a QR code, browse the menu, and order/pay from their phone directly — this is the dominant commercial pattern for QR ordering, but it is explicitly a payment-inclusive flow (order + pay), which is NOT the pattern Shop AI uses. — [Toast support](https://support.toasttab.com/en/article/Setting-up-mobile-payments-and-digital-menus); [Toast support, QR code types](https://support.toasttab.com/en/article/Generating-QR-Codes-for-Menus-and-Online-Ordering)
- Some tablet-based restaurant ordering systems include a dedicated "call a waiter" or "request server assistance" action separate from order submission, letting guests signal for service or make special requests without it being a payment action (aggregated across several vendor-guide descriptions found in search results; no single primary source pinned down beyond these vendor summaries).
- A pre-digital, low-tech precedent for staff-flagging exists and is documented: some restaurants use physical flag poles or a red/green chip system at the table specifically to signal "need attention" vs. "leave us alone," entirely decoupled from ordering or payment. — [Signal v. Noise (37signals)](https://signalvnoise.com/archives2/restaurant_flagging)
- "Contactless dining" as a category (Wikipedia overview) documents QR ordering broadly but does not itself distinguish payment-based from non-payment "assistant" flows in named products. — [Wikipedia, Contactless dining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contactless_dining)

### Inferences
- Because no source describes a named "picks list, no payment" pattern from a major app, Shop AI's model is somewhat novel relative to the mainstream QR-ordering category (which is dominated by Toast/Square-style pay-enabled flows). The safest adaptation is to borrow wishlist UX conventions (persistent, low-friction, editable, no urgency messaging) for the "My picks" list itself, while borrowing the physical-restaurant "flag for staff" precedent for the actual hand-off moment — i.e., "My picks" should behave like a running note diners curate calmly (wishlist-style: easy add/remove, quantity as a simple stepper, no checkout-style progress bar or "expires soon" urgency), and the moment of showing it to staff should be framed as "show/tell your waiter" rather than "submit order," since there is no backend order object being transacted.
- Since Shop AI already separates "My picks" from "Call staff" as two distinct floating-bar buttons, this maps reasonably well onto the wishlist-vs-cart intent split (planning/curation vs. active service request) found in the NN/g source — this is a reasonable existing design decision, not something to change based on this research.
- Quantity adjustment on a non-payment picks list should likely stay a simple stepper (+/-) since there's no order total or payment math riding on precision — but no source specifically validated this for a non-checkout context; treat as a low-confidence inference rather than a cited finding.

### Gaps
- No case study or article was found describing a QR-menu product that offers a "flag items for staff, no payment" experience by name (searches for GoDine and Feedify specifically returned no substantive results — see Gaps in the final section below). This is a genuine blind spot: the closest available research (wishlist/cart, physical flagging) is adjacent, not directly on-point, and should be flagged to the dev team as such rather than presented as a proven pattern.
- No source addresses how to word a "picks" list's confirmation state (e.g., "Ready to show your waiter?") — this is untested territory per available research.

## Empty states, sold-out items, and loading states in a menu list

### Takeaway
Empty states should state clearly why the list is empty and offer a direct next action (never leave a blank screen); sold-out items should be marked immediately and obviously rather than left orderable until a failure moment, since stale availability is explicitly called out as trust-eroding.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g's three guidelines for empty-state design: (1) communicate system status clearly and never show a message that contradicts what loads moments later — "users lose trust when told 'No records' only to see content appear seconds later"; (2) use the empty state as a teaching moment (e.g., "Star your favorites to list them here") rather than leaving a blank container that reads as an error; (3) provide a direct, clickable pathway for the relevant task rather than vague instructions that say what to do but not how. — [NN/g, "Designing Empty States in Complex Applications"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/)
- Food-app-specific empty-state guidance (from an aggregated design-pattern summary): use food-themed illustration or photography and surface curated suggestions (popular/trending items) rather than a generic "add your first item" prompt, since curated suggestions "convert exploration into action much faster." — [Pencil & Paper](https://www.pencilandpaper.io/articles/empty-states) (general empty-state guidance; the food-app example is cited within an aggregated search summary, not verified against the primary article directly)
- Sold-out handling: make "sold out" messaging obvious without requiring the user to tap into an item to discover it; some implementations dynamically label sold-out variants inline (e.g., within a dropdown) rather than after selection; visual badges/stickers on the item itself are used so unavailability is visible at a glance; and a common pattern is to gray out/disable the unavailable option entirely rather than letting users select it and only then showing an error, since post-hoc error messages create friction. — [UX Beginner](https://uxbeginner.com/ecommerce-ux-design-sell-out/); [Medium/UX School](https://medium.com/ux-school/how-to-not-sell-out-in-ux-design-b256bdd525) (both e-commerce-general, not food-specific, but directly transferable to a "sold out dish" card state)
- QR-menu-specific guidance frames stale availability as a severe trust problem: "remove 86'd items immediately or mark unavailable" and out-of-date availability is described as "one of the most trust-eroding moments" for a digital menu specifically. — [Dineazi](https://dineazi.com/resources/qr-menu-design-tips)
- General mobile filtering-benchmark research (500+ examples cataloged) documents that mobile filter/list UIs commonly struggle with "the necessity for multiple trips back-and-forth between product lists and separate filtering interfaces" and "difficulties with overlong lists of filter options" as failure modes distinct from empty/sold-out states but relevant to list usability generally. — [Baymard, mobile filtering options benchmark](https://baymard.com/mcommerce-usability/benchmark/mobile-page-types/filtering-options)

### Inferences
- For Shop AI's dish list, a sold-out `DishCard` should likely be visually de-emphasized (dimmed/badge) but still visible and tappable-to-view (so diners aren't confused about whether a dish simply doesn't exist vs. is temporarily unavailable), consistent with "make status obvious" rather than hiding the item outright — though no source directly addressed "hide vs. show-dimmed" for sold-out items in a non-purchase context; this is an inference from the "disable rather than let users hit an error" and "remove 86'd items immediately" findings taken together.
- An empty state for Shop AI's search/filter results (e.g., "no vegan mains match") should follow NN/g's three-part structure: explain why (filters applied), teach (what filters are active / how to clear them), and offer a direct action (a visible "clear filters" or "see all dishes" button) rather than a bare "No results" message.
- An empty "My picks" list is a first-run empty state candidate for the "curated suggestions" pattern (e.g., surfacing specials/popular dishes) rather than a blank "nothing here yet."

### Gaps
- No source specifically covered loading-state UX (skeleton screens, spinners) for a menu list — searches did not surface a citable, food-specific loading-state best practice; this remains an open gap and should not be inferred beyond generic web-performance guidance already covered in the mobile-browsing section above (2–3 second load target).

## Combining a browsable menu with an embedded AI assistant on one screen

### Takeaway
A fixed, bottom-right floating action button is the dominant, expected pattern for surfacing a chat/assistant entry point without hiding it in a menu, precisely because users "instinctively look for chat widgets there" — this generally favors a floating button over a separate tab for a persistent assistant.

### Cited Findings
- A floating action button (FAB) "is a circled icon floating above the user interface. Its shape, position, and color ensure it stands out from the rest of a UI." — [Mobbin, FAB glossary](https://mobbin.com/glossary/floating-action-button)
- For chatbots specifically, "a visible floating action button positioned in a fixed location (typically bottom right) serves as a better pattern than burying the chat interface in a settings menu," and is typically placed "in a consistent spot on every relevant page ... tapping into user familiarity as people instinctively look for chat widgets there." — [Widget Chat](https://widget-chat.com/blog/chatbot-ui-design-best-practices/)
- The chatbot's presence should be "constant but not obstructive" — a fixed/floating position that stays on-screen through scrolling, so the entry point is always reachable, using a contrasting color or distinct icon so it isn't overlooked. — [Eyaana](https://eyaana.com/blogs/uiux-design-optimizations-make-the-chatbot-noticeable-and-inviting/)
- Broader conversational-AI UX guidance (from a customer-service/self-service context, not food-specific) stresses balancing autonomous AI action with user control, keeping the assistant UI minimalist and focused, and being transparent about what the assistant can do — this is general guidance, not specific to a menu+chat combination screen. — [PROS/Ascend UX](https://pros.com/ascend/conversational-ai-next-generation-user-experience/); [WillowTree/TELUS Digital, 7 UX/UI rules for conversational AI](https://www.willowtreeapps.com/insights/willowtrees-7-ux-ui-rules-for-designing-a-conversational-ai-assistant)

### Inferences
- Shop AI's current pattern — a floating bottom bar with "My picks," "Ask the waiter," and "Call staff" as persistent buttons rather than a separate tab — matches the cited FAB convention directly (fixed position, always reachable, not buried in a menu). This is validated by the sources rather than something to change.
- Because the sources emphasize "constant but not obstructive," the three-button floating bar should be checked for whether it obscures the bottom of the dish list or dish-card tap targets at 390px — the "not obstructive" requirement is a testable constraint even though no source measured it numerically for a 3-button bar specifically.

### Gaps
- No food-delivery-specific or QR-menu-specific source was found addressing the exact question of tabs vs. floating buttons for a browsing+assistant combination screen — the FAB findings above are drawn from general chatbot-UX sources, not from a restaurant app case study, so the applicability, while reasonable, is an extrapolation rather than a directly-confirmed food-app pattern.
- No source discussed how multiple floating actions (3, in Shop AI's case, vs. the single-FAB pattern typically described) should be laid out relative to each other (e.g., expandable FAB "speed dial" vs. a persistent bar) — this is a genuine gap; the sources found only address single-purpose FABs.

## What leading food-ordering/menu apps do well for category/filter navigation and dish presentation

### Takeaway
Concrete, citable specifics from named major apps were limited by paywalls and thin public write-ups; the most solid citable findings are: Grubhub's dietary-filter wording/editability research, Deliveroo's dietary-filter breadth, and Toast's QR-code-type separation (menu-only vs. order vs. payment codes) — DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grab, foodpanda, GoDine, and Feedify yielded either paywalled, unconfirmed, or no substantive results and should not be cited beyond what's below.

### Cited Findings
- Grubhub: dietary filters are integrated into the app's pre-browse search flow, are editable directly from the filter menu, support saving multiple dietary-preference sets, and use "free"-suffixed wording (e.g., "Milk-free") to remove ambiguity. — [Silvia Diff](https://www.silviadiff.com/grubhub-dietary-filters)
- Deliveroo: has "a comprehensive yet easy to navigate" dietary filter system with clear visual indicators for vegetarian, vegan, gluten-free, and other nutritional requirements, plus an advanced search that filters by name, cuisine, dietary preference, or delivery time simultaneously. — [Addictaco](https://addictaco.com/best-ux-practices-for-food-delivery-apps/) (secondary/aggregator source; original Deliveroo design documentation was not located, so this claim rests on an aggregator, not a primary source)
- Toast: separates QR codes by function — a Menu QR Code (view only), an Order QR Code (place orders), and a Payment QR Code (pay the check) — rather than one code doing everything; this is a direct, citable precedent for "one QR code, one job" that Shop AI already implicitly follows by having no payment code at all. — [Toast support](https://support.toasttab.com/en/article/Generating-QR-Codes-for-Menus-and-Online-Ordering)
- DoorDash: a UX case study confirms a menu-navigation frustration was found in 5/5 interviewed users, motivating an IA redesign — but the specific fix could not be retrieved (403 on full article). — [UX Collective, search-snippet only](https://uxdesign.cc/doordash-redesign-a-ux-case-study-559370cac697)
- Uber Eats: Baymard's benchmark treats "Food Menu," "Filtering + Restaurant List," "Sorting Tool," and "Product Page" as separate, purpose-built screens (398 elements catalogued) — confirming a structural pattern of separating browse/filter/sort/detail — but specific critiques are paywalled. — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/ux-benchmark/case-studies/uber-eats)
- Grab and foodpanda: no substantive, citable, primary-source findings were retrieved. A foodpanda redesign case study exists on Medium but was only seen as a search snippet, not fetched in full, so no specific claim from it is included here.
- GoDine and Feedify: searches returned no results specifically matching these named products — general QR-menu-app feature lists (multi-language support, table/takeaway/kiosk modes, mobile payment integrations, allergen info sharing) were found, but none tied to "GoDine" or "Feedify" by name with confidence. — [general QR-menu feature summary, no product-specific source](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.iaproject.qrtomenu&hl=en_US)

### Inferences
- The clearest transferable pattern across all named apps found is: separate concerns (menu view vs. order vs. payment vs. filter vs. sort) into distinct, purpose-built UI rather than one overloaded screen or code — Shop AI's existing separation of search/category/dietary filters, dish list, detail modal, and the 3-button floating bar (picks/ask/call) already follows this precedent.
- Grubhub's wording and save-multiple-preference-sets findings are the most concretely actionable items from this section for Shop AI's dietary pills.

### Gaps
- GoDine and Feedify (named in the assignment's suggested sources) returned no usable, citable results — this should be reported as a clear gap, not papered over. If the dev team specifically wants these two vendors' patterns, a follow-up research pass hitting their own marketing/product pages directly (rather than general search) would be needed.
- Grab and foodpanda: only a search-snippet-level foodpanda case study was found; no Grab-specific menu/filter case study was found at all. Both should be treated as unresearched for this report.
- DoorDash's and Uber Eats' specific design solutions (beyond the problem statements/screen taxonomy above) were blocked by a 403 error and a paywall respectively — the report reflects only what was publicly retrievable.
