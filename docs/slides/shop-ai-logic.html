The problem and the product

Problem (M1 charter, SRS)

Small restaurants have few front-of-house staff. At peak hours they must take orders and answer the same questions about ingredients, allergens, opening hours and recommendations. Result: slow service, mistakes, repeated questions.

Evidence: three interviews in Chiang Rai (front-of-house employee, noodle-shop chef-owner, fried-food-shop owner).

Product

  • Diner: scans the table QR, no app, no account. Menu, filters, allergy profile, AI chat in Thai, Burmese or English, picks, call staff, table bill.
  • Owner: menu and 14 allergens, photo import, hours, FAQ, AI settings, QR codes, staff, insights.
  • Waiter and chef: live floor and kitchen screens behind a PIN.

The one design principle

Anything that can hurt a customer or cost the restaurant money is answered by code and the database, with fixed sentences. The language model is the last resort, and its answer is checked before anyone sees it.

Rules answer (milliseconds)

  • allergens and ingredients
  • prices, opening hours
  • sold out, alternatives
  • orders, the bill, call staff
  • "I can't confirm, please ask the staff"

Model answers (about 5 s)

  • recommendations in free words
  • FAQ-style and small talk
  • everything the rules did not recognise

Then: pattern checks on the reply. If it fails, the diner gets a safe sentence, never the model's text.

Why

  • A wrong allergy answer is dangerous and a legal risk.
  • A model can be fluently wrong.
  • Rules are testable: 1,293 automated tests, 100% on the rule-based questions.

How the rules understand a sentence

Language detection

Count characters in the Burmese and Thai Unicode blocks. Burmese wins ties, Thai if any Thai, Latin letters mean English, otherwise the language the diner picked in the app. Burmese digits (၁၀၀) become 100 so budgets work.

Allergen words

Keyword lists per allergen in English, Thai and Burmese (shrimp, prawn, กุ้ง, ပုစွန် all mean shellfish). English uses word boundaries so "nut" does not match "coconut". A word inside a dish name ("Shrimp Pad Thai") is not an allergy statement: the dish name is blanked out first.

Dish matching

Each dish gets many names: full name in 3 languages, without brackets, the last word, words unique to that dish, short Thai forms. A word counts only if it is unique to one dish and is not an allergen word. The longest match wins ("Thai Iced Tea" over "Tea"). Quantity from "2 x", "x2", "2 จาน", clamped 1 to 20.

Voice

The owner picks male or female. Thai gets ครับ or ค่ะ, Burmese gets the polite spoken style (ပါတယ်), never the formal written one.

Missing data is never "none"

In the database allergens = NULL means not provided, and [] means none recorded. Every allergen answer names the not-provided dishes separately and ends with "please confirm with the staff". It never says "safe".

The language model path and its guards

What the model gets

  • The restaurant's data as JSON: dishes, prices, allergens, tags, availability, hours, FAQs, specials
  • Rules in the prompt: answer only from this data, same language, at most 4 sentences, never "safe"
  • Only the last turns of history (every token costs seconds)
  • It may end with ACTION: {show_dishes}; the ids are filtered to real, available dishes, at most 4

If any check fails

A recommendation question gets a few real dishes from the database (that fit the allergy profile). Anything else gets "I don't have that information, please ask the staff". The model's text is thrown away.

Checks on the reply (checkModelReply)

blockEmpty, or only "how can I help?"
blockUnsafe wording: "is safe", "perfectly safe", "harmless", "no risk", "allergen-free", "guarantee", Thai ปลอดภัย, Burmese ဘေးကင်း
blockClaims "no allergens" (any language) → replaced by the database allergen answer
blockA price that is not the dish's real price; an amount that is not on the menu
blockA dish name that does not exist (capitalised names with unknown words)
blockSays a sold-out dish is available
blockTalks about allergens at all (the rules own that topic)

Honest limit: these are pattern checks, a last line of defence and not a proof. On 28 Oct "perfectly safe" got through the old pattern; it is fixed and tested, but a paraphrase nobody listed could still pass. That is why allergen facts never depend on the model.

Allergen data: unknown, estimated, verified

New this week. A list of allergens is only as good as the recipe check behind it, so the system now records who stands behind each list.

unknown

No list. Shown to diners as "not provided". Never verifiable. Hidden from diners with an allergy profile.

estimated

A list exists, nobody at the restaurant compared it with the recipe and supplier labels. The default for imports, seeds and AI-read menus. Diners see "an estimate, ask staff".

verified

The owner ticked "I compared this list with the kitchen recipe and the supplier labels today". Date and e-mail stored. Diners see "checked by the restaurant on …".

Rules enforced on the server

  • Verified only on an explicit owner request, and only when a list exists.
  • Editing the allergens or the ingredients drops it back to estimated: a check of an old recipe says nothing about the new one.
  • A check older than 180 days stops counting (a policy choice; the guidance only says "regularly").
  • The owner's e-mail is stored for the record and is never sent to diners.

Tests: allergen-status.test.ts. Not built yet: supplier documents, per-ingredient records, change history, cross-contact field.

What the research says about "verified"

Law and guidance

  • EU Reg. 1169/2011: since 2014 the 14 allergens must be given for restaurant food too, in writing (FSAI).
  • UK FSA best practice: written information and a conversation; keep records of ingredients and recipes; keep information "accurate and up to date at all times"; check whether ingredients changed; limit last-minute substitutions; suppliers must report allergen changes.
  • Thailand: the rules I found (MOPH Notification 450, effective 19 Jul 2024) are for prepacked food. I found no equivalent duty for restaurant menus. A 2026 draft would add sesame, celery, mustard, lupin; it is not law.

Why estimates are risky here

  • Shrimp paste in curry paste, fish sauce, oyster sauce (mollusc), peanut oil: allergens that the dish name does not show.
  • Burmese ngapi is fermented fish or shrimp paste; peanut and sesame oils are common.
  • A supplier swap can change allergens while the packaging looks the same.

About AI

  • A 2025 JACI study (via AAAAI): critical errors in about 5% of ChatGPT allergy answers.
  • A thesis that extracted allergens from restaurant sites got F1 = 68%: fine for a warning, not for a guarantee.

Say it honestly

Desk research, mostly secondary sources; the full legal texts were not read, and no study measuring restaurant-menu allergen accuracy was found. The 180-day interval is my design choice. Open the primary links in the research file before quoting a number.

Allergies travel with the order

The diner's profile and the allergens named in the chat go onto the ticket. Each dish line is flagged when it lists one of them, or when it has no allergen data ("unknown").

The bill without accounts

Diners have no login. The phone holds a random 192-bit receipt code issued by the server with its first order. It opens the table's bill only after staff accepted an order carrying it; before that the phone sees its own picks only (fixed 28 Oct: anyone could read another table's bill).

Input rules

Whole quantities only, repeated dishes added then capped at 20, at most 50 lines, table required, double-tap guarded.

Design, accessibility and languages

Visual direction

Monochrome "liquid glass": black, white and gray chrome, translucent surfaces, soft shadows, water-like motion. Food photographs keep their own colour. The old coloured banner and card grid were removed. A research-based redesign: Nielsen Norman heuristics and WCAG 2.2 rules guided the changes.

Accessibility rules

  • WCAG AA contrast, controls at least 44 px, visible focus
  • Reduced-motion and reduced-transparency fallbacks
  • Page language attribute follows the chosen language
  • Thai and Myanmar fonts, script-aware wrapping

Three clearly separate actions

Ask AI (information), Call staff (a person comes), Send to staff (picks for a waiter to accept). The interface never says "ordered" when it only means "sent".

Languages

The UI and AI answer in the language of the question. Burmese keeps "Allergens" in English by project convention and uses one fixed staff sentence. Open item: the Burmese wording, including the new allergen-status lines, needs a native speaker's review.

Conditions that matter

  • The model and the app share one computer. In the 5 Oct run, with a build, tests and a browser running at the same time, the median rose to 12.6 s and only 63% finished within 15 s. Close other programs during the demo.
  • The first question after the model has been idle can take about 15 s and fall back. Warm it up before the exam.
  • The prompt grows with the menu: the numbers are for a small menu.
  • Of the 100 questions, 63 reached the model and 37 were answered by the rules (the new source column shows it): rules now cover more than on 5 Oct.
  • Burmese open answers are the slowest: the Burmese "not on the menu" eval question hit the 15 s cutoff in 2 of 2 runs.

Not measured

  • Phone browser time over Wi-Fi
  • Menu-photo import accuracy
  • Owner onboarding time

Honest limitations

Safety and data

  • All allergen lists are estimated; nobody has checked a real kitchen. The system now says so; it cannot make the data true.
  • The reply guard is a pattern list, not a proof.
  • Staff order tickets flag listed allergens and "unknown" but not "estimated".
  • Free chat text is stored as typed; no redaction or retention job.

Engineering

  • Strict TypeScript is off; many handlers use any.
  • Owner login still uses the older check-then-count limit.
  • One process: queue and limits are not shared across servers.
  • Money is a floating-point column.

Evidence

  • No usability test with real diners or staff.
  • Not tested on a real Windows laptop or a phone over Wi-Fi.
  • Burmese open questions can reach the 15 s cutoff (safe fallback shown).
  • Burmese strings need a native speaker.

Next steps, in order

  1. Check real recipes with a kitchen and mark them verified
  2. Supplier documents and change history per dish
  3. Mention the status in the AI's allergen sentence (needs a re-run of the evals)
  4. Moderated test with 5 diners and 2 staff

Questions to expect

Can the AI tell me a dish is safe?
No. Allergen facts come from the database, never the model, and every answer ends with "confirm with the staff". The model's text is pattern-checked as a last line of defence; I list that as a limit.
What if the owner's allergen data is wrong?
Then the answers are wrong, so the system labels every list as unknown, estimated or verified, resets a check when the recipe changes, and expires it after 180 days.
Why a local model?
Customer questions never leave the restaurant, no per-question cost. The price is speed (about 5 s) and one request at a time.
Why rules and not the model for everything?
Rules are instant, testable and can't invent. The model only gets what rules can't recognise.
Is there a legal duty in Thailand?
I found rules for prepacked food (Notification 450). I did not find a duty for restaurant menus. I used the EU and UK expectations as the benchmark.
What did you find wrong in your own project?
Twelve audited defects; for example a table's bill readable with an invented pick, and parallel PIN guesses bypassing the lockout. All fixed with regression tests.
What is not tested?
Real phones, real Windows laptops, real kitchens, usability with real people, photo-import accuracy.
What would you do next?
Verify real recipes with a kitchen, add supplier records and a change history, then run a small moderated test.
